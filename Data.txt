Konfer data model
=================

This describes where data lives and why, not how any one file happens to be written. Where the code and this file disagree,
one of the two is wrong and it is worth working out which before changing either.


1.  Three homes, and the test for which
---------------------------------------

Every piece of state in the mod belongs in exactly one of three places, and which one is decidable per variable without
understanding the code around it.

konfer.*
  Mod wide and durable. The same for every policy, survives a raid, survives a change of active policy. The saved variables
  and everything derived from them, the one window, and the handful of that window's widgets other files reach.

konfer.policies[handle].*
  One per policy. The config that policy currently has in hand, the frames of the panels it contributes to the window's
  pages, and whatever it works out for itself. An inactive policy still holds all of it, so that switching back to a policy
  lands where it was left.

konfer.raid.*
  Exists once because only one raid, under one active policy and its one config, can be running. The loot session,
  reservations, the undo stack, who has been warned about what, who of the config is here (uidmap), who of the raid the
  config has never heard of (missing, nmissing), which disenchanters are online (denchers), what is on the corpse
  (bossloot), and where the game has no master loot, who collects the group's loot and who has consented to it
  (collecting, collector, consent, consents, asked; GroupLoot.lua). What the collecting leader owes by trade is Kore's,
  KLD.trades, saved with the character (K.chardb.trades), so that it outlives the raid along with the items in their bags.

The test is "would wiping this when the raid ends be correct?". If yes it is raid state, even though it is saved. If it survives a raid but differs
per policy it belongs on the policy. If it survives everything and is the same for all policies it stays on konfer.

konfer.raid is one table rather than a scattering of variables so that ending a raid or changing the active policy is

  konfer:EmptyRaid()

and is complete by construction. A field missed out of a dozen separate resets is yesterday leaking into today. A field
missed out of this one does not exist. It is emptied and never set to nil, because every member is reached as
konfer.raid.something and much of the mod asks whether one of them is there at all. A change of config in the same group
keeps what belongs to the group rather than the config: whether becoming master looter has been put to the player, and who
collects the group's loot with whose consent.

A raid is a group, and the group is the game's. Kore keeps the id the game gives the player's own group (KRP.group_id), which
the game says only as the group is formed, joined and left, so Kore saves it to know it again after a reload, and forgets it
the moment the player is found in no group. konfer.raid is that group's record and is saved with it (konfer.chardb.raid),
so a reload in the same group, or a login back into it after the connection dropped, starts from where it was. A record
for any other group, or for none, is thrown away, and so is what was on the corpse, a reload having closed the loot window.
The record is only ever a starting point and never outranks the game: whatever the game says about the group is read from
the game, whatever is counted from the group is counted again on the next walk of it, and what the raid has agreed is
asked for again and replaced by the answer.

A policy's own record of a raid that has to outlive the night's group, PUG's +1s and decay and its reserves from softres.it,
which its co-admins share and its raiders see, is the policy's data on the config and carries the group's id, so that a
login back into the same group carries on and one anywhere else asks.

Some of these are remembered and some are counted afresh on every walk of the group, and that difference decides nothing
about where they live. Both are about this raid and both go when it does.

What it does decide is what travels. konfer.raidshared names the keys the whole raid agrees on rather than works out for
itself, which today is who is reserved and which three are disenchanting. Those are decisions about tonight, so REQRS asks
for them on joining, and again when the config loot runs against changes, a switch of config or of policy, and ACKRS
carries the lot. Everything else is derived from this client's own view of the group, and
sending one client's view to another that can see the group perfectly well itself would be worse than useless. A key
belongs in raidshared only when it is something somebody decided.

ACKRS carries the whole picture and replaces ours with it, rather than adding two half pictures together. A key the answer
does not name is cleared, because a reservation left standing only because nobody mentioned it is exactly the bug the other
way round would have. It is sent whether or not anything is set, since silence would otherwise be indistinguishable from
nobody having answered and would leave whoever asked holding what was left over from the last raid.

One client answers, not every admin. konfer:RaidSettingsAnswerer picks the highest ranked person in the raid, and every
client works that out the same way from what it can already see, so nobody has to agree it first. The order is the guild
master, the config's owner, whoever leads the raid or assists, and last the master looter. konfer:MaySetRaidSetting draws
the line for changing anything above the master looter: the people who set a raid up did it for whoever ends up running
loot, and are frequently not in the raid at all to be overruled.

Tonight's disenchanters are konfer.MAX_DENCHERS and never more. They start as the config's and are changed for the night by
konfer:SetRaidDencher, which does not touch cfg.settings.denchers. Who a config's enchanters are is a config admin's to say;
who is doing it tonight is the raid's.

uidmap is the one that looks like it might belong elsewhere, because a uid is a config's and means nothing outside the
config that issued it. It is in here rather than on the config precisely because of that: it maps the raid going on now
onto the config running it, so it is neither the raid's alone nor the config's, and it is wrong the moment either changes.


2.  Configurations
------------------

Each policy's configs live in its own konfer.frdb[handle].configs, keyed by config id, invisible to every other policy.
konfer:PolicyDB and konfer:PolicyConfigs reach them. A config id means nothing at mod level, and konfer:Config(cfgid)
searching every policy is a convenience for code handed a bare id, not the model.

Nothing needs one flat table. A message reaches only the endpoint of the policy that sent it, under that policy's own prefix,
so the policy a config id belongs to is a fact of its arrival and never a search. The foyer spans policies one at a time, by
selection, and asks the selected policy for its configs.

Anything taking a config id or a user id is therefore a policy method, pol:Setting(which, cfgid) rather than
konfer:X(which, cfgid), and mod level code talks about names and asks each policy in turn. The platform services a policy is
given look their config up within that policy, so a policy handed some other policy's id gets nil rather than an answer.

cfg.policy says which policy a config runs, and is the handle of the policy whose table holds it. It is fixed when the config
is created and never offered as a choice afterwards, because a config is made from inside a policy and that is what decides
it. There is no policy chooser anywhere.

Config ids are minted by KC.CreateNewID(extra) in Kore, a CRC over the clock to the second, a counter separating ids issued
in the same second, the player's own name, and a fourth value the caller supplies. Konfer supplies the policy handle, so two
policies handed identical clock, counter and player still draw from separate spaces. What a config is called never
participates. Two admins who both create "Main Raid" are creating two different things and must get two ids.

Three fields of a config are deliberately independent and none is derived from another.

  cfg.policy    which distribution system it runs
  cfg.channel   where its admins sync, guild or the group
  cfg.owner     who may rename it, change its channel, or hand it on

cfg.channel is read only by a policy's own CSendAM and CSendChannelAM, which are Konfer's. Kore is a transport and never
reads a config. In a group CSendAM sends to that group whatever the channel, so everybody raiding together hears the config's
events and its loot whatever their guild, and another raid of the same guild hears none of it. Out of a group, guild means
every admin is a guildmate and hears it. The sync request, the users refresh and the loot history broadcast go by the
channel in a group too (CSendChannelAM). An admin of a group config who is not in that group sees nothing until they are.

A config's loot history is each client's own, raiders' as well as admins', each keeping and trimming it. It travels with no
config. A row goes to everybody holding the config as it is written (LHADD), and the master looter may broadcast the whole
of it (LHBCS), at most once every konfer.HISTORY_BCAST_GAP seconds, the receiver adding the rows it does not hold. An admin
keeps every row and takes a broadcast as it comes. A raider who is no admin keeps rows only where they asked to on the
Options page (konfer.chardb.recordhistory, per character, off unless they do), it being a great deal to keep unasked, and is
asked about each broadcast unless they have answered Never (konfer.chardb.ignorehistory, a box on the same page).
cfg.histsent is when this client last broadcast it, kept on the config so the gap outlasts a reload. One arriving to replace
a config we hold keeps the history and histsent we had for it, and one never held before starts with none.
konfer:KeepLocalConfigState is the one place that says so.

Its settings are not each client's own. What a config does is its owner's to decide, so they travel with it, in the c field
of a broadcast, and replace whatever the receiver had. A change between admins is a CHSET on the event stream carrying the
whole of them rather than the one that moved.

That is why they are small. A config keeps them as two strings, cfg.opts holding one character per yes-or-no setting and
cfg.vals the rest with a colon between them, in the order konfer.copts and konfer.cvals declare. That declared order IS the
storage format, so those two lists are only ever appended to: inserting into one reinterprets every config already saved
and every one in flight, and a setting that goes away keeps its slot at a cost of one character. A change the format cannot
absorb by appending is a dbversion convert in Utility.lua.

Nothing outside konfer:MakeConfig knows any of that. cfg.settings is a table with no contents of its own and a metatable
that reads a character out of one string and writes it back, so a caller still says cfg.settings.auto_bid. A metatable is
not saved, which is the point of the two strings being real fields, and is also why every config that comes back out of the
saved variables has to be made an object again before anything reads it. MakeConfig puts the proxy back along with the
config's own methods, at creation, in konfer:SetConfig for anything arriving from another client, and over the lot at
startup as konfer:MakeAllConfigs, because a config that missed one reads every setting as nil without saying so.

Two of those settings carry guild rank filtering, which is the platform's and which every policy has. rank_filter says
whether the config filters by guild rank at all, and rank_order gives each of the guild's ranks a place from 1 to
konfer.MAX_RANK_ORDER. Ranks sharing a place are equivalent and a filter naming any one of them admits all of them, a
guild's rank list being an ordering of people by office where a raid wants one by standing, and the two agreeing only by
luck. A rank the order says nothing about stands at 1, so an order nobody has touched puts the whole guild level and a
filter run against it admits every member of it. The order is where the guild says which of its ranks outrank which, and
until somebody says so nothing guesses: reading the rank list as an ordering by standing is the very assumption the order
exists to replace. Only cfg:RankOrder, RankPermits, NextRank and RankFiltering know the order exists.

A filter itself is not a setting. It lives where it applies: def_rank on a roll list, rank on an item, and the live one the
master looter holds for the item being distributed, which is neither and is remembered nowhere.

def_list, the roll list a drop is bid on when nothing else names one, is the platform's too, and is edited in the Options
panel on the Admin tab beside the rank ones. Roll lists are the platform's, so which of a config's lists is the fallback is
a fact about those lists rather than about how any policy hands things out. It offers the lists of the config the tab has
selected rather than the one the window has in hand.

A policy's own settings are not in cfg.settings, and like the platform's they are the owner's and travel with the config,
in the p field of a broadcast beside the two strings in c. konfer.defaults and cfg.settings are the platform's and are the whole list
of what every policy has; a policy declares its own in its descriptor's defaults, they live in cfg.psettings keyed by its
handle, and it reads and writes them with pol:Setting and pol:ChangeSetting. A change to one travels as PSSET where a platform setting
travels as CHSET, and PSSET carries the policy's whole block for the same reason CHSET carries both strings. What is in
that block is checked against what the policy declares, so a name it has never heard of or a value of the wrong kind is
dropped and the rest is still taken, and a setting the sender never mentioned lands on its default rather than keeping
whatever this client had. Two tables and two commands, so that neither side has to know what is in the other's and a policy
may name a setting whatever it likes.

The block is left named rather than packed into a string the way the platform's is. A positional format is a contract that
an external policy has to keep, and an author who reordered their own list would silently reinterpret every config they had
ever saved. Suicide Kings has three settings; the compaction is earned for the platform's thirty and is not earned here.

The block is made from the defaults the first time it is asked for rather than when the config is, so a policy registered
later, or one that declares a new setting, has it in every config that already existed. Keyed by handle although a config
belongs to exactly one policy, so that a config which changed hands keeps each policy's answers apart.

Suicide Kings holds suicide_rolls, bid_threshold and try_roll. The last two are edited on the platform's Loot tab,
because what quality of item is worth distributing and what happens when nobody bids are questions every policy has to
answer even though the answer is the answering policy's.

A policy's own data on a config is the other thing it keeps there, for what is not a setting: a collection edited an entry
at a time, EP/GP's and DKP's awards. It declares it in its descriptor's configdata, it lives in cfg.pdata keyed by its
handle, and it is read with cfg:PolicyData. It is whole only when the config travels whole, in a broadcast, a full sync, a
recovery or a config copy, checked against what the policy declares as a setting is. Between admins it changes only by the
policy's own events, one entry each, so PSSET never carries it: a whole block sent for one setting would carry the sender's
copy of every entry with it and undo an entry another admin had just changed.

A policy's corner of the saved variables, konfer:PolicyDB, holds its configs and which of them it had in hand at logout,
and may hold beside them whatever else of the policy's belongs to this machine alone and must never travel with a config,
though no policy keeps anything there at present. Nothing of the platform's reads that or sends it, a broadcast, a sync and
a copy included: it travels only by the policy's own messages, and those only to whoever it is for.

A policy's own options on a roll list are the same thing one level down. It declares them in its descriptor's listoptions,
they live on the list in rl.poptions keyed by its handle, and it reads them with pol:ListOption. They are edited on the
Lists page's Config tab beside the list's own options and travel with them: the tab's one CHLST carries the policy's whole
block after the platform's fields, a config travelling whole carries it in each list's row, and a config copy carries it.
What arrives is checked against what the policy declares, as a setting is, and an option the sender left out lands on its
default. A copied list has them because a copy is the whole list.

A config's checksum is a running XOR over its event stream rather than a hash of its contents, so adding storage is sync safe
provided every mutation goes through AddEvent. Which also means the fold and the event have to happen together: an event
folded in and then dropped leaves two admins with matching event ids and different checksums, and nothing recovers from that
but a full sync.


3.  Users, lists and standings
------------------------------

What is true of a character is Kore's, what a config thinks of them is that config's, and what a player privately thinks of
them is that player's and never leaves the machine.

K.frdb.users, read as KU.users, holds every character this faction and realm knows about, keyed by the canonical
Name-Realm. It is Kore's rather than this mod's because a character's class, race, level, specs and professions are facts
about the game's own characters and are the same facts whichever mod asked, so every KahLua mod on the client reads and
writes the one table rather than each keeping a copy that drifts. A record is a positional array through the USER_
constants, documented in Kore/UserDB.lua, and its first slot is the character's KUID. The role a character plays is not
stored at all, being what their spec plays.

One thing on that record is the player's own opinion rather than fact, and it is never sent: their notes, ratings included.
Everything the game itself can prove travels as the WUSER_ wire row, and is offered rather than written when it arrives,
a sender being able to be honestly wrong about any of it.

A config keeps cfg.uids[uid], positional through the CFGUSER_ constants, documented in Konfer.lua. The KUID reaches the
character's record and everything after it is this config's own view of that user, whether they are a raid enchanter here,
whether their place on these lists is frozen, and pdata, whatever the config's policy keeps about them, which the platform
stores and sends whole and never looks inside. The four hex uid CreateNewUser allocates is an admin id prefix plus a
sequence, so admins who are offline from each other cannot collide, and that scheme only works because a config has a
defined admin list to allocate from. One character in two configs has two entries and two uids, and one record.

A person has three names, and each is for one thing. The Name, the canonical Name-Realm, is the game's and is all two
clients share about somebody when no config is in common. The KUID is this client's own: four hex digits Kore issues from
K.frdb.nextkuid, never used twice, and never sent anywhere. Kore keys its records by Name and holds the KUID in each, and
KU.kuids turns a KUID back into the Name, rebuilt at login and never saved. The uid is a config's: issued by one of its
admins and the same in every copy of it, while each copy's entry points at its own client's KUID. So two clients that number
the same people quite differently still agree on every uid, and that agreement is all their traffic needs.

Everything about a person, Kore's record, the notes, the ratings and the alt link, is keyed by KUID, and so a rename moves
the record's key and touches nothing that points at it. Everything about a config, its lists, standings, admins, bidders,
rollers, reservations, disenchanters, the loot history, the undo stack and every event on the wire, is keyed and compared by
uid. A character name appears only where something outside insists on one: a game API, a whisper, an announcement in chat,
a line on screen, and another client. Each of those turns a name into a KUID or a uid at the doorway, KU:Find, KU:Add and
cfg:FindUser, and never carries one further in. A config arriving whole names its people by Name, there being nothing else
the two clients share, and they become this client's KUIDs only as it is accepted, so a config refused adds nobody. Two
tables are keyed by name on purpose, konfer.raid.missing and the warning sets beside it, because what they hold is
precisely the people the config has issued no uid for.

There is one alt link and it is a fact. A human has one main and every other character of theirs is an alt of it, so the
link is one level deep and is the same wherever that person appears. Nobody is an alt in one raid and a main in the next,
and anybody wanting two mains simply does not link them. It lives once, in USER_ALTOF and USER_ALTS as KUIDs, written only
by KU:SetMain. Nobody is their own alt, nobody is an alt of an alt, and somebody who already has alts cannot become one,
and that one function is the only place those rules exist. A config carries no link of its own.

The link is not a fact the game can prove and it is not an opinion either. It is what the people running a raid say about
the human behind two characters, so it is neither in the WUSER_ row nor offered. It travels beside that row, in CHUSR 'A',
MDUSR and each user's row in a broadcast or a sync, and a receiver writes it as it stands.

The two config events carry a uid and a broadcast carries a character name, because they are answering different questions.
CHUSR 'A' and MDUSR happen inside a config both ends hold, where the main must be a member for anything to be done about
them. A whole config arriving may name an alt whose main that config has never held, which a uid cannot express, so the
user row carries the name and the broadcast's m table carries the wire row for any such main. They arrive as people, with
no uid and no membership. Neither ever carries a KUID.

Only one thing clears a link, CHUSR 'A' with onoff false, which is an admin unlinking somebody by hand. Everywhere else an
empty main is silence rather than a denial. MDUSR's flag comes from cfg:IsAlt, which answers only about mains this config
holds, so a false there says the sender could not see one and never that there is none.

What a config supplies is the uid it knows a character by. cfg:IsAlt and cfg:UserAlts read the one link and answer in
that config's uids, and that is what tethering, suicides and awards run on. Marking somebody an alt needs their main to be
in that config, which is why cfg:SetUserIsAlt takes uids for both and why the Users page offers that config's mains and
no others. Kore's editor has no config in hand and offers every main there is.

A character whose main is not in a config stands as a main in that config, and is still that main's alt everywhere, the
link being untouched. Adding the main to the config is all it takes for the two to tether there.

Nobody is added passively. Creating a config, creating a user, importing the guild, adding who is missing from the raid, and
accepting a broadcast or a sync are the only ways a character arrives. What another admin says about a character already
known is an offer, put to the player one tick per user in Kore/UserOffers.lua, and a refusal is not remembered, the
comparison always being against what is stored. Somebody who will not stop asking is K.frdb.blockedsenders, and then nothing
they broadcast is even unpacked. A character's own answer about itself is the exception and is taken as it stands, that being
the one answer nobody has to be asked about.

A user outlives every config that has them, and is only ever forgotten deliberately, in Kore's user editor, once no config
has an entry for them. Kore knows nothing about configs, so it asks: KU:RegisterConsumer registers UserConfigs, which is
what refuses a delete, and Konfer answers it by walking its policies. A rename asks nothing, every config's entry pointing
at the KUID the rename leaves alone.

Whether an award belongs to the human or to the character is the list's tethering rather than a shared fact about a player.
A tethered list holds mains, so what is won or earned on it is the person's; on one that is not, each character stands on
its own.

Standings are per list, never per config. The ordered rl.users array is canonical, and for Suicide Kings the index in it is
the standing. Policies that need numbers use the platform owned rl.standings beside it, keyed by uid, with the fields the
policy declared in StandingFields. Created on demand rather than by a database upgrade, the checksum being over the event
stream and not over the contents.

What a policy did to a list's numbers that the loot history does not record, EP/GP's and DKP's awards of points and their
decays, and DKP's zero-sum shares of what a winner spent, is rl.journal, beside the standings. The platform keeps it with
the list and never reads it, and a copied list has it because a copy is the whole list under another name. The policy
fills it from its own events, so every admin's copy is filled alike, and carries it in its ListInfo for a config that
travels whole. Both keep every decay and a month of everything else, each naming the admin who made it and the uids it
reached, and show them on the Lists page's Journal tab.

When a list was last decayed is EP/GP's and DKP's rl.lastdecay, beside the journal and carried the same way, set by the
decay event on every admin alike. It is what says when the list may be decayed again, once in five days, and it is a field
of its own rather than read out of the journal, which is a record to show and not a rule to go by.

Beside each member's numbers in rl.standings, EP/GP and DKP keep that member's lifetime totals on the list: earned, everything
awarded, penalties taking it down; charged, everything charged for loot, an undone charge taking it back; and decayed_<field>,
what decay has taken off each number that decays. Nothing decays them, and only awards, charges and decay move them; numbers
set by hand, or brought by somebody put on a list, move the numbers alone. They are a list's, so a config's Friday run is kept
apart from its main raid by being a list of its own, and they travel with the numbers wherever those do, in IMLST and in a
config travelling whole. Every admin moves them from the same events, which is why they agree.

A decay event carries any base a number decays towards, EP/GP's base GP, as the admin who decayed held it, so that an admin
holding another base still decays alike. Base GP itself is kept out of the stored GP, which decays as GP plus the base would,
less the base and never below nothing: the original EP/GP's decay without the base being stored.

Roll lists, their membership, filters, rank restrictions, alt tethering and extra list are platform. Only the ordering
semantics layered on top belong to a policy.


4.  Policies
------------

A policy is not an addon. It registers a descriptor with konfer:RegisterPolicy() and is keyed in konfer.policies by its
handle. Policy.lua documents the descriptor in full.

The handle is the policy's identity in four ways at once. It is what cfg.policy stores, what keys konfer.policies, the prefix
on every frame the policy owns, and the tail of its prefix on the wire. pol.prefix is derived from it as handle .. "_" at
registration rather than declared, so a policy cannot ship a prefix that disagrees with its handle or forget one, and a
duplicate handle is an error at registration rather than a silent overwrite. It is the handle and never the command, because
a command is what a user types and is subject to translation.

Registration happens at load, and every policy registers whether or not it is switched off, so the engine knows all of them
and can resolve any config's policy.

Its panels are built separately, by konfer:BuildPolicyUI(pp), which guards twice: a policy that is switched off builds
nothing, and one already built hands back what it has, so a caller may be careless and call it as often as it likes. When
that happens is konfer.EAGER_POLICY_UI. Lazily, on first activation, is what ships, and a policy nobody switches to costs
nothing at all. Eager is for development, so that a fault in a policy's Construct shows up at login rather than weeks later.

Building is deferred rather than done at load because WoW cannot destroy a frame. Panels built for a policy the user has
switched off would sit there for the rest of the session, and switching one back on has to hand back the frames it already
made rather than leak a second set.

A policy owns presentation and ordering. It gets no private database namespace and no wire protocol of its own, though it
does own which events it answers and the prefix they arrive under. Suicide Kings, EP/GP and DKP are views over one shared
model and differ in how the guild is asked to think about list order, not in what is stored.

Where the platform calls into a policy it goes through konfer:PolicyCall, which returns the platform default for a method
the policy does not implement and reports once per method per session for one that raises. An incomplete or broken policy
degrades rather than breaking loot distribution for everybody, which is the discipline Kore applies to addon callbacks with
safecall.


5.  Switched off policies
-------------------------

A policy that is switched off is still loaded, still registered, and GetPolicy still resolves its configs. Forgetting which
policy governs a config would lose the data rather than shelve it, which is the opposite of what switching one off means.

Its configs are never removed unless instructed. They are ignored.

Ignored means ignored everywhere it is observable. The foyer does not list the policy so its configs cannot be reached, its
slash command refuses and says where to switch it back on, and comms drops messages naming one of its configs at the
dispatch gate, before any handler sees them, so a switched off policy costs no bandwidth and writes nothing.

Two scans deliberately do not filter. Name uniqueness checks every config including shelved ones, or re-enabling one turns up
a duplicate name. Database validation and upgrade walk every config, or a shelved one comes back broken.

A policy that is switched off can never be the active one at startup, so where the one remembered is off, the first policy
still on takes its place.


6.  The active policy
---------------------

Exactly one policy is active, and konfer.apol is that policy object, with its current config at konfer.apol.cfg and the id at
konfer.apol.currentid. Those two are one fact in two fields and are written only by konfer:SetPolicyCurrent, which is what
keeps them from disagreeing.

Active means two things and they always travel together. It is the policy the window is showing, and it is the policy that
arms for loot against whichever config it has in hand. Which policy it is is a mod level decision and a policy gets no say in
it.

konfer:ActivatePolicy is the only thing that changes it, and every route to a switch goes through there. It puts the outgoing
policy away before it sets the incoming one up, so the two never have their panels on screen together, and it refuses
outright while loot is being handed out, there being no wording that makes abandoning a distribution halfway a reasonable
thing to click through. The refusal is there rather than at the routes because one of them, the foyer's Activate, asks
nothing first by design, and a switch nobody was asked about is still a switch.

A policy is told it has become or stopped being the active one through its descriptor's OnActivate(onoff), and through
nothing else. Only the two policies it changes for are told. Walking the registry would say the same fact five times and
make four policies work out from an argument that nothing about them has changed.

Deactivation is not being switched off. An inactive policy stays registered on the wire, still takes broadcasts and sync, and
still owns its configs and its standings. It is simply not on screen and does not arm for loot, so OnActivate(false) must not
throw away anything a switch back needs.

There is no mod wide current config. A current config is meaningful only inside a policy, and an inactive policy goes on
holding the one it last had so that switching back lands where it was left. cfg:SetDefault sets a policy's current
config and nothing else, with one settled exception: while the active policy has no config at all, a policy that gains or
picks one becomes active. That cannot yank the window out from under anybody, because a policy with no config refuses to open
at all.


7.  Windows
-----------

There are exactly two windows, plus the settings and user editor dialogs. The foyer, and the policy window, konfer.mainwin,
frame KonferMainWin. Only one of the two is up at a time and showing either puts the other away.

There is one policy window and it shows the active policy. Exactly one policy is active, so a second window could only ever
show what the first one already does, and five of them was five copies of every page in the mod with one of them ever in use.
The window is the mod's, built once by konfer:InitialiseMainWindow.

The foyer is the mod as a whole and is the one piece of UI that spans policies, which it does one at a time by selection.
Clicking a policy card fills the top right with only that policy's configs and puts the policy's description at the top of
the bottom right, and selecting one of those configs fills the rest of it with only that config: the platform's box for each
of its lists, or whatever the policy draws itself through PopulateBannerInfo.

The pages, Lists, Raid, Loot, Users, Sync, Config and Options, are therefore the mod's and are built once. What a policy
contributes is panels within those pages, through the descriptor hooks ListsPage, RaidPage, ItemsPage, UsersPage and
CreateConfigUI, and only the
active policy's are shown. Each page keeps a slot for them; a policy's frames go in it, hidden, and the slot is sized and
titled per policy as one comes up. What is genuinely common the platform builds and a policy adds its own beside it. There is
no forced template and no assembling of one tree from many policies.

Widget handles live in one table per page file, plus konfer.qf for the handful of the window's widgets that other files
reach. They are the window's and not a policy's, because there is one window. A policy's own frames hang off the policy,
pp.listsframe and the rest, because that is what they belong to.

A scroll list row, or any callback built once and run later, reads konfer.apol when it runs. A policy captured at build time
is whichever policy happened to be active then, and the window outlives that. The same goes for a policy's own vocabulary:
pol:Str() is asked when the words are put on screen, not when the frame holding them was made.

Frame names are prefixed with the handle for everything a policy builds. Everything KUI derives from a window's name follows
automatically once the window is named, so pages, tabs, content frames, topbars and unnamed splits cost nothing. Explicit
names, which is every dropdown, scroll list, hand named split, dialog and scroll list item button, take the prefix
themselves. Two collisions there are silent rather than loud, because KUI.ddframes caches a dropdown's menu by name and
scroll list item buttons are recycled by name.


8.  The wire
------------

Two things are versioned independently and a bump of one must never force a bump of the other.

KC.WIRE_VERSION is the envelope, the framing, checksum and serialisation. It is Kore's, shared by every Kore addon, and is
the version letter every message starts with, upper case when the payload was compressed. Nothing ever parses past a
version letter it does not implement.

konfer.protocol is the addon's events and their arguments. It is one flat number. Policies ship inside Konfer so both ends of
a conversation update together, and there is nothing for compatibility code to bridge. A message from a higher protocol than
ours is refused and tells us we are out of date. Anything at or below is accepted.

Every policy is its own endpoint, and so is the mod, all under the mod's one prefix, KONFER. Every message's payload begins
with the protocol, the endpoint that sent it (a policy's handle, or * for the mod), the command and the config it is about,
and the mod's dispatcher routes on the endpoint, so Suicide Kings is handed only Suicide Kings messages. None of that is
Kore's, which carries a payload it never looks into. A policy sends its own messages, and the mod's own SendAM carries only
the mod's own.

Which events a policy answers is its own. RegisterPolicy gives every policy its own copy of the standard immediate and
recorded handler sets, copied at registration rather than left to the policy because a policy that forgot would answer
nothing at all, and the policy's own Comms.lua then adds, replaces or removes entries without touching anybody else's.

Event names stay the policy's. SULST and SUNDO, not AWARD. An award means something different in every policy and carries
different arguments, so a shared name is a collision waiting for the second policy that needs one.

konfer.BCAST_VERSION versions a config broadcast, which is a whole config in one message rather than an event. A receiver
reads it to decide whether it understands the message at all, and never decodes one shape as though it were another.

Nothing arriving is trusted to be well formed. A message names its own admins, its own lists and its own users, and a field
pointing at something the same message did not name is a message that disagrees with itself. It is dropped rather than
followed, because the thing it points at is exactly what cannot be invented.


9.  Loot
--------

Konfer decides, Kore delivers. Any assumption that an item can be assigned belongs in KLD. Konfer's vocabulary is who should
get an item, never how it reaches them. Which is how retail gets a Konfer at all, there being no master looter there and loot
landing in somebody's bags for them to trade.

Deciding and delivering are two events and delivery can fail. An award is never modelled as instantaneous or certain. The
descriptor's Award, undo token and UndoAward contract is what expresses this. A trade that never happens is an undo.

A win is decided once: when bids close or a roll ends with a winner, or when the master looter goes through with an item's
own instructions, and in a policy with costing when they pick the charge in the costing box. The award, its history row and
the announcement are written then, and stand whether or not the item then arrives. The item leaves the loot list whatever
becomes of it. Where the game is asked to hand it over, it leaves when the game says the slot has cleared, because the game
answers nothing when asked and a winner with full bags or out of range simply never receives it.

Authority to run loot is being an admin of the config, never KRP.is_ml. The master looter check may gate delivery. It must
never gate a decision.

The order the loot is in is the order the loot window shows it. KLD hands its items to a consumer in loot slot order and
Konfer's list follows, so that what the master looter is reading and what they are clicking agree.

How bidders are ranked is the policy's, through RankBidders in its descriptor, and a policy that keeps numbers breaks its own
ties. For Suicide Kings bidders are ordered by position on the list and nothing else. Where a bidder stands is the whole of
what it has to say about who should win, so there is nothing to break a tie with and no tie to break, because two people
cannot occupy one position.
